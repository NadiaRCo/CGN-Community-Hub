🌼 W1 · Growing with the Land: Native Plants and Your Food Garden
When / Where

(Wed) July 8, 7pm – 8:30pm · Online, via Zoom

Description

Did you know that native plants and food gardening are more connected than most of us realise?

Many of us think of native plants and food growing as two separate gardening priorities - one for wildlife and pollinators, and one for nourishment. This workshop will show you how they are, in fact, deeply interdependent and how native plants are critical in sustaining habitats, ecosystems, and your garden.

Join us for an engaging session with Cynthia J. Benjamin, Vice-Chair of the Ottawa Wildflower Seed Library, who will guide you through the specific native plants that can directly support, strengthen, and boon your food garden.

This workshop is appropriate for all levels of gardeners. Whether you are a home grower, balcony gardener, or community garden member, you will leave with practical knowledge on how to grow with the land - not just on it.

In this workshop, you will learn:

  • Which native plants to grow to support your food production, and why they work
  • How native plants support pollinators, soil health, and biodiversity in ways that benefit your edible garden
  • How to identify real native plants and where to source them responsibly in Ottawa
  • Simple first steps for introducing native plants into your existing growing space, no matter how big or small

Just Food's Community Gardening Network is committed to programming that reflects our responsibilities as settlers and inhabitants of Turtle Island. Growing native plants is one small, grounded way to reconnect with the land and honour the (food) knowledge of the Algonquin Anishinaabe peoples who have been the stewards of these lands for millennia, and on whose unceded territory we garden. Just Food is committed to be a part of the ongoing work towards Truth and Reconciliation.

About your Facilitator — Cynthia J. Benjamin, M.A.

Cynthia is Vice-Chair of the Ottawa Wildflower Seed Library (OWSL), a grassroots organization dedicated to providing free access to native seeds, teaching responsible gardening, and building community around native plant growing.

With 35 years of experience in strategic planning, training, and community development, Cynthia brings a wealth of energy and real-world knowledge in her passion to preserve and nurture the natural world. One of her most beloved projects was spearheading the "Inspiration Playground/Community Garden" initiative from 2012 to 2017, which transformed over 7,000 square feet of pavement into an outdoor classroom, community garden, native plant and pollinator garden, and playground. It drew hundreds of volunteers and Cynthia received two House of Commons Volunteer Awards (2016 and 2017) for this work. Today, the space continues to thrive.

The workshop is $10 or Pay-What-You-Can (PWYC) even if that's $0! Everyone is welcome to attend regardless of cost. If you wish to send an e-transfer before the session, please send your payment to financials@justfood.ca and indicate the name of the workshop in the "notes" section.

No matter how carefully you plan or how experienced a gardener you are, every garden will face its share of challenges each season. 🌽 W4 · Intensive Intercropping and Companion Planting
When / Where

(Wed) July 29, 7:00PM – 8:30PM · Online, via Zoom

Description

In recognition of the International Day of Friendship on July 30, this workshop will explore what friendship and relationship can look like in the garden.

Through companion planting, intensive intercropping, and polyculture, we can make better use of growing space while supporting healthier plants, greater biodiversity, and more resilient garden ecosystems. Rather than growing each crop in isolation, these approaches consider how plants can share space, provide shade or structure, attract pollinators and beneficial insects, reduce pest pressure, and support one another as they grow.

These approaches begin with a simple recognition: a garden is shaped by relationships. Plants interact not only with one another, but also with soil, insects, animals, people, and the wider ecosystem. The Three Sisters—corn, beans, and squash grown together—offer a well-known example of relational planting, with each plant contributing to the shared growing system.

Join Cole Etherington, a Métis farmer and founder of The Good Shit, for a practical session on designing intentional plant communities and bringing these principles into your own garden. Cole will share examples from his regenerative growing practices and offer practical ideas for growing more food in the space you have.

This workshop is intended for gardeners who are comfortable with the basics and want to expand their approach, but curious beginners are always welcome. 🍀

About Your Facilitator — Cole Etherington, The Good Shit

Cole Etherington is the founder of The Good Shit, a social enterprise dedicated to helping people and the planet, one organic garden at a time. With a professional background in sociology and knowledge translation, along with a passion for equity, food access, and climate action, Cole brings a unique perspective to the landscape of sustainable, resilient gardening.

Cole's gardening journey started with chickens 🐔. He noticed how their manure helped to rebuild the soil and thus the variety of plants that soil could nurture. The chickens eventually got rabbit 🐇 and goat 🐐 friends and that symbiotic relationship continues: What they leave behind feeds the soil. The soil feeds the plants. The plants feed people. Nothing is wasted. Everything is connected.

Over time, it became clear to Cole that sustainability is not only about how we grow, but who gets to grow. So The Good Garden was launched in 2025, as a nonprofit arm focused on community-led growing, education, and food access initiatives - to increase everyone's access to growing food. Because caring for the land also means caring for one another.

Workshop Description

Fermentation Extends Life! of our harvests in many food traditions. Fermenting is one of the simplest ways to preserve the harvest, reduce waste, and make good use of seasonal gluts in Ontario's short growing season.

There are many other techniques of vegetable fermenting other than sauerkraut and kimchi, and there are many other vegetables other than cabbage and Napa that are used in fermentation. Dry fermentation is one of these that go beyond sauerkraut and Kimchi, and is used in the eastern Himalayan mountain region.

Join us in the catering kitchen at the Just Food Community Farm for this hands-on workshop, on how to ferment your many surplus and less desirable vegetables at the end of the season.

Li Bo of Chi Garden will guide participants through the fermentation process step by step, using organic semi-dried vegetables, with herbs and spices. Each participant will pack their own jar and take home their freshly made ferment to enjoy later!

All materials will be provided, including new mason jars and the vegetables, salt, ginger, and spices. There is no need to bring anything.

About your Facilitator

Li Bo is a farmer, environmentalist, and co-founder of Chi Garden Ecological Farm, a small ecological urban farm at the Just Food Community Farm in Ottawa. Since 2017 he and his partner Sun Shan have grown food and cared for the land through regenerative nature-based practices that build a living soil food web, support resilient plants and nutrient dense harvests.

Fermentation is at the heart of Chi Garden's work. Li Bo and Sun Shan preserve much of their harvest through lacto-fermentation, offering 20+ cultured pickles year-round, and have been teaching fermentation widely since 2019 — on farms, in community spaces, online, and with cultural groups, seniors, and BIPOC communities. Li Bo is especially passionate about East Asian fermentation traditions, including techniques from his home province of Yunnan, and about bridging and rediscovering healthful food and nature-friendly farming traditions from both East and West. Through cultivating diversity, Chi Garden farms is a place for conversation, cultural connection, and imagining shared futures.

🔎 What the research showed

Coordinator trainings elsewhere (NC State, Illinois Extension, Cornell, the Garden Stewardship Institute and the ACGA’s Growing Communities curriculum) lean towards the “people” side of gardens — leadership, volunteers, rules, communication, inclusion — plus planning and funding, more than growing techniques. I found no 2026–27 winter series for Ontario garden coordinators, so there may be a gap Just Food can fill; I did not find training specifically on conflict resolution for garden leaders, so that one would need a facilitator. Most of the examples are US programs, so adapt them to Ottawa and Just Food’s remit.

🌍 What good garden-network coordinators do

Gathered from public sources on garden leadership and network support. Most are U.S. city and Extension programs, so adapt them to Ottawa and to Just Food’s remit rather than copying them.

Delegate so leaders grow

Seattle’s P-Patch leadership handbook calls delegation the chief tool for training new garden leaders. For you: when a coordinator asks for help, offer a template or a short call that builds their capacity rather than doing the task for them.

→ Seattle P-Patch Leadership Handbook
Be transparent and keep contact regular

A Virginia Tech study found inclusive, transparent communication marks well-led gardens, and an extension study found self-sufficient gardens had leaders who communicated often with gardeners and stakeholders. For you: a predictable rhythm (e.g. a short seasonal check-in per garden) beats occasional big asks.

→ Virginia Tech garden leadership study
Ask realistically — leaders are already stretched

A food systems journal study notes garden leaders already carry heavy responsibilities, so loading more on them often fails. For you: keep asks small and specific (one survey, one date) and say what is in it for them.

→ Journal of Agriculture, Food Systems, and Community Development
Site visits and peer mentors

UF/IFAS describes one-on-one site visits plus continued mentorship from other gardeners as how new gardens get supported. For you: pair a new garden with an experienced coordinator and visit once early in the season.

→ UF/IFAS Extension
Create chances for leaders to meet each other

A USDA SNAP-Ed story notes garden leaders rarely get chances to connect and share practices. For you: the April meet & greet and occasional coordinator-only calls fit this; ask coordinators what they would like to swap.

→ USDA SNAP-Ed success story
Learn from other networks

Denver Urban Gardens presented its centralised network model (land, water, insurance, recruiting and supporting garden leaders) at the 2025 national conference; ACGA promotes participatory leader development; Cornell Cooperative Extension runs a Community Garden Leadership Program.

→ Denver Urban Gardens
Build champions, not just attendees

One Extension coordinator’s advice was to find community partners willing to champion the network so it is not driven by one organisation. For you: identify two or three garden coordinators who could co-host, speak or pull in neighbours.

→ Cornell Cooperative Extension

🌼 W1 · Growing with the Land: Native Plants and Your Food Garden
When / Where

(Wed) July 8, 7pm – 8:30pm · Online, via Zoom

Description

Did you know that native plants and food gardening are more connected than most of us realise?

Many of us think of native plants and food growing as two separate gardening priorities - one for wildlife and pollinators, and one for nourishment. This workshop will show you how they are, in fact, deeply interdependent and how native plants are critical in sustaining habitats, ecosystems, and your garden.

Join us for an engaging session with Cynthia J. Benjamin, Vice-Chair of the Ottawa Wildflower Seed Library, who will guide you through the specific native plants that can directly support, strengthen, and boon your food garden.

This workshop is appropriate for all levels of gardeners. Whether you are a home grower, balcony gardener, or community garden member, you will leave with practical knowledge on how to grow with the land - not just on it.

In this workshop, you will learn:

  • Which native plants to grow to support your food production, and why they work
  • How native plants support pollinators, soil health, and biodiversity in ways that benefit your edible garden
  • How to identify real native plants and where to source them responsibly in Ottawa
  • Simple first steps for introducing native plants into your existing growing space, no matter how big or small

Just Food's Community Gardening Network is committed to programming that reflects our responsibilities as settlers and inhabitants of Turtle Island. Growing native plants is one small, grounded way to reconnect with the land and honour the (food) knowledge of the Algonquin Anishinaabe peoples who have been the stewards of these lands for millennia, and on whose unceded territory we garden. Just Food is committed to be a part of the ongoing work towards Truth and Reconciliation.

About your Facilitator — Cynthia J. Benjamin, M.A.

Cynthia is Vice-Chair of the Ottawa Wildflower Seed Library (OWSL), a grassroots organization dedicated to providing free access to native seeds, teaching responsible gardening, and building community around native plant growing.

With 35 years of experience in strategic planning, training, and community development, Cynthia brings a wealth of energy and real-world knowledge in her passion to preserve and nurture the natural world. 🌽 W4 · Intensive Intercropping and Companion Planting
When / Where

(Wed) July 29, 7:00PM – 8:30PM · Online, via Zoom

Description

In recognition of the International Day of Friendship on July 30, this workshop will explore what friendship and relationship can look like in the garden.

Through companion planting, intensive intercropping, and polyculture, we can make better use of growing space while supporting healthier plants, greater biodiversity, and more resilient garden ecosystems. Rather than growing each crop in isolation, these approaches consider how plants can share space, provide shade or structure, attract pollinators and beneficial insects, reduce pest pressure, and support one another as they grow.

These approaches begin with a simple recognition: a garden is shaped by relationships. Plants interact not only with one another, but also with soil, insects, animals, people, and the wider ecosystem. The Three Sisters—corn, beans, and squash grown together—offer a well-known example of relational planting, with each plant contributing to the shared growing system.

Join Cole Etherington, a Métis farmer and founder of The Good Shit, for a practical session on designing intentional plant communities and bringing these principles into your own garden. 📖 The Full Strategy — "Grow Together" (working version)

Prepared by Nadia Rangira · Jul 23, 2026 · internal working document — click each part to expand. Amendments pending.

Part 1 · What We Know About Our Audience

Our workshop attendees are home growers, balcony gardeners, community plot holders, and renters in Ottawa and region — primarily beginner to intermediate. They care about sustainability, food sovereignty, and community, not just "tips and tricks." The audience is ethnically and linguistically diverse (French programming is a priority), and many are already connected to gardens in the CGN network.

What makes them show up: practical, hands-on knowledge they can use immediately · connection to a community of people like them · accessible cost (PWYC removes the barrier) · a speaker or topic they feel personally drawn to · feeling part of something ongoing — not a one-off event.

Part 2 · The Core Problem & the "Grow Together" Reframe

Right now, workshops can feel like individual events: someone attends one, maybe comes back, maybe doesn't. There's no strong thread connecting them to the next workshop, to each other, or to the broader CGN.

The reframe: the CGN is a community of growers, growing together. Every workshop is a chapter in that story, every facilitator a community member, every attendee a contributor. So: not "sign up for a workshop" but "join us for the next session in our growing season series." Not one-off promotions, but a running editorial calendar with recurring formats.

Part 3 · The Seven Tactical Pillars

1 · Series identity: name each series, create the full-lineup graphic first, treat workshops as chapters people can anticipate.

2 · Anticipation, not announcements: the sequence per workshop — teaser (2 wks) → full announcement (1 wk) → countdown (2–3 days) → day-of stories → day-after recap.

3 · Post-workshop community building: recaps with key takeaways · "what's one thing you'll try?" prompts · share slides with Q&A highlights · tag and thank the facilitator publicly.

4 · Newsletter as community anchor: a dedicated CGN section in every issue — upcoming workshop + seasonal tip + a community garden story — keeping CGN top of mind between workshops.

5 · Participant involvement: topic polls ("what do you want to learn this fall?") · a community question at each workshop gathered from registrants in advance.

6 · Facilitator as community member: "Meet the Facilitator" personal-format posts · post-workshop reflections · public acknowledgment in recaps.

7 · Coordinator cross-promotion: copy-paste-friendly blasts coordinators can forward to their gardeners — the highest-trust channel in the network.

Part 4 · Metrics to Track

After each workshop and each series: registrations (Zoom/form count) · attendance vs registrations · social reach and engagement rate · newsletter open rate · attendee retention (% attending more than one) · new signups from workshops ("how did you hear about us?").

Now live: the Promotion Impact Board (Workshops tab) tracks sign-ups against every send — the Jul 28 blast produced 19 sign-ups in 10 hours vs a 1/day no-promotion baseline.

Part 5 · Planning Integration (for every future series)

Bake the strategy in from the start: 1) name the series and theme before announcing workshops · 2) create the full-lineup graphic as the first content piece · 3) draft the 3-post sequence per workshop at planning stage · 4) solicit topic ideas from the community before finalizing · 5) schedule everything in Loomly in advance · 6) articulate the community thread in the opening newsletter.

Part 6 · Proposal: a dedicated CGN Instagram 🌟

The idea: a standalone CGN Instagram (Facebook later, if the pilot proves out) — CGN's own discoverable home for gardening tips, workshop news, and member spotlights, without competing for space on the main JF account.

Why it works: someone who only cares about community gardening currently has no single place to follow. A dedicated account can post more casually and often — what's growing this week, a member's harvest — and directly serves the "Grow Together" goal of a community that anticipates what we post.

Considerations for Kate: ownership and sustainability (now viable with Nadia staying on) · content differentiation from the main JF account · governance — posts still flow through Kate's review · cross-promotion between the two accounts.

Thank you and take care,
Nadia & The CGN Team

💡 Engagement boosters: add a one-line hook before the greeting, and: "Know a gardener who'd love this? Forward this email — everyone's welcome."

✉️ Template — Email 2 · 1 day before · 1:30 PM

Subject: Reminder - Tomorrow! Upcoming Zoom Workshop with Just Food

Same structure as Email 1 with "happening Tomorrow ([Day])!" — and differentiate it: add a teaser line such as "Come ready with a question about your own garden — [Facilitator] will be taking live questions."

✉️ Template — Email 3 · day of · 9:00 AM

Subject: Reminder - Zoom Workshop at 7pm! [Workshop Title]

Shortest of the four: "happening tonight at 7:00–8:30 pm!" — Zoom details box first, one energising line ("Bring a question about your own garden!"), costs note, sign-off. Dial-in numbers can wait for Email 4.

✉️ Template — Email 4 · day of · 6:30 PM (fixed)

Subject: ZOOM WORKSHOP DETAILS: [Workshop Title]

"Happening in 30 minutes!" — Zoom box front and centre with all dial-ins, facilitator line, costs note. Nice touch: "We'll open the room at 6:50 pm — join early to say hello!"
